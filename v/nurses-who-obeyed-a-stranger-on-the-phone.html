Nurses Who Obeyed a Stranger on the Phone

In a famous hospital experiment almost every nurse was ready to give a dangerous dose on a phone call from an unknown doctor.

Transcript

Twenty one out of twenty two nurses were ready to give a dangerous dose because an unknown doctor said so.

In nineteen sixty six, Charles Hofling arranged for a man posing as a doctor to call nurses at night.

He told them to give a patient twice the maximum dose of an unfamiliar drug. Hospital rules forbade phone orders.

Almost every nurse went to give it, before being stopped by the researchers.

It became a classic example of obedience to authority.

But in nineteen seventy seven, a similar study used a familiar drug and let nurses talk to colleagues first. Most refused.

The lesson is not that people blindly obey. It is that rushed, isolated people, facing a title, stop questioning.

When an authority pressures you to act fast and alone, slow down and ask someone else.

The science

Based on obedience to authority in hospitals (Hofling et al., 1966; Rank and Jacobson, 1977).

Hofling, C. K., Brotzman, E., Dalrymple, S., Graves, N., & Pierce, C. M. (1966). An experimental study in nurse-physician relationships. Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease, 143(2), 171-180. Rank, S. G., & Jacobson, C. K. (1977). Hospital nurses' compliance with medication overdose orders: A failure to replicate. Journal of Health and Social Behavior, 18(2), 188-193 (2 of 18 complied).
