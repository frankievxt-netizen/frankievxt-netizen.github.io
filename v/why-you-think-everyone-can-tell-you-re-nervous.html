Why You Think Everyone Can Tell You're Nervous

Liars expected nearly half of their listeners to catch them, but only about a quarter did.

Transcript

Your heart is pounding, your voice feels shaky, and you are sure everyone in the room can see it.

In nineteen ninety eight, Thomas Gilovich, Kenneth Savitsky and Victoria Medvec called this the illusion of transparency.

In one game at Cornell, students took turns lying to their small group.

Liars expected nearly half the group to catch them. Only about a quarter did, no better than chance.

Your feelings are loud inside you, so you assume they leak out just as loudly.

In two thousand three, Savitsky and Gilovich found that nervous public speakers also overestimated how anxious they looked.

Then some speakers learned about this illusion before speaking. They felt better, and observers rated their speeches higher.

So when you feel exposed, remember. People see far less than you feel.

The science

Based on the illusion of transparency (Gilovich, Savitsky and Medvec, 1998; Savitsky and Gilovich, 2003).

Gilovich, T., Savitsky, K., & Medvec, V. H. (1998). The illusion of transparency: Biased assessments of others' ability to read one's emotional states. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 75(2), 332-346 (Study 1a: 39 Cornell students; liars predicted 48.8% detection, actual 25.6%, indistinguishable from chance). Savitsky, K., & Gilovich, T. (2003). The illusion of transparency and the alleviation of speech anxiety. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 39(6), 618-625 (speakers told about the illusion rated their speeches better and were rated better by observers). Sample sizes for the 2003 study were not confirmed. Education only.
