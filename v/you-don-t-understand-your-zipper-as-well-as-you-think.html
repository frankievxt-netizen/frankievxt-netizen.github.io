You Don't Understand Your Zipper as Well as You Think

People feel sure they understand how everyday objects work, until they are asked to explain them step by step.

Transcript

You think you know how a zipper works. Try explaining it.

In two thousand two, Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil asked people to rate how well they understood everyday things, like zippers, toilets and locks.

Most felt fairly confident.

Then they had to write a detailed, step by step explanation.

Afterward, their confidence dropped sharply. They realized they knew much less than they thought.

This is the illusion of explanatory depth. Being familiar with something feels like understanding it.

Later research found the same with political opinions. Explaining how a policy works made people less extreme.

Before you argue about something, try to explain how it works. You may change your own mind first.

The science

Based on the illusion of explanatory depth (Rozenblit and Keil, 2002).

Rozenblit, L., & Keil, F. (2002). The misunderstood limits of folk science: An illusion of explanatory depth. Cognitive Science, 26(5), 521-562. Political extension: Fernbach, Rogers, Fox & Sloman (2013), Psychological Science, 24(6), 939-946.
