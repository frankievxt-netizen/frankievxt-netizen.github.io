Your Clearest Memory Is Probably Wrong

The memories you are most sure about can be the ones that changed the most.

Transcript

The memory you are most certain about might be the one you got most wrong.

The morning after the Challenger space shuttle exploded in nineteen eighty six, Ulric Neisser asked students where they were when they heard.

About two and a half years later, he asked them again.

Many stories had completely changed. Different place, different people, different moment.

Yet people were very confident. Some insisted their old written answer must be the wrong one.

These are called flashbulb memories. Big emotional moments feel sharp, so you trust them, but they fade and change like any other memory.

A study after September eleventh found the same thing. Accuracy dropped, confidence stayed high.

Feeling sure is not the same as being right.

The science

Based on flashbulb memories (Neisser and Harsch, 1992; Talarico and Rubin, 2003).

Neisser, U., & Harsch, N. (1992). Phantom flashbulbs: False recollections of hearing the news about Challenger. In Affect and Accuracy in Recall. Talarico, J. M., & Rubin, D. C. (2003). Confidence, not consistency, characterizes flashbulb memories. Psychological Science, 14(5), 455-461.
