Why Your Explanations Only Make Sense to You

If you tap a famous song on a table, you will be shocked by how few people can guess it.

Transcript

Tap a famous song on a table and you think people will guess it. They will not.

In nineteen ninety, a Stanford student named Elizabeth Newton turned this into an experiment.

Tappers chose songs like Happy Birthday and predicted listeners would guess half of them.

Listeners guessed three songs out of one hundred and twenty.

The tappers could hear the melody in their heads. The listeners only heard knocking.

This is the curse of knowledge. Once you know something, you cannot imagine not knowing it.

It is why your instructions feel obvious to you, and your coworker still gets them wrong.

So when someone does not get it, they may not be slow. You might just be tapping.

The science

Based on the curse of knowledge (Newton, 1990).

Newton, L. (1990). Overconfidence in the communication of intent: Heard and unheard melodies. Doctoral dissertation, Stanford University. Popularized in Heath & Heath, Made to Stick (2007). Term from Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber (1989).
