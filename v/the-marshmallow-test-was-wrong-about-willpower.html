The Marshmallow Test Was Wrong About Willpower

The famous marshmallow test was said to predict success, but a bigger study found something else matters more.

Transcript

You have heard of the marshmallow test. The kids who waited became more successful. It is not that simple.

In the late nineteen sixties, Walter Mischel offered children one marshmallow now, or two if they waited.

Years later, the kids who waited seemed to do better in school.

The lesson became: willpower in childhood predicts your future.

But the original kids were mostly from families at Stanford University.

In twenty eighteen, Tyler Watts and his team repeated it with a much larger and more diverse group. The link became much weaker, and mostly disappeared after accounting for family background and early skills.

Waiting makes sense when you trust the second marshmallow will really come. Many children have learned it might not.

Sometimes what looks like weak willpower is just a reasonable response to an uncertain world.

The science

Based on the marshmallow test (Mischel, 1972; Watts, Duncan and Quan, 2018).

Mischel, W., Ebbesen, E. B., & Raskoff Zeiss, A. (1972). Cognitive and attentional mechanisms in delay of gratification. JPSP, 21(2), 204-218. Watts, T. W., Duncan, G. J., & Quan, H. (2018). Revisiting the marshmallow test. Psychological Science, 29(7), 1159-1177. Trust: Kidd, Palmeri & Aslin (2013), Cognition.
