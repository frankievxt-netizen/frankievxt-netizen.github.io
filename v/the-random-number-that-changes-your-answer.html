The Random Number That Changes Your Answer

A completely random number can change your answer to a question you think you are judging carefully.

Transcript

A random number can change an answer you think you chose carefully.

Researchers asked people whether Gandhi died before or after the age of nine.

Everyone knew that was ridiculous. Then they guessed his real age at death.

Another group was asked about the age of one hundred and forty. Their guesses came out much higher.

This is anchoring. The first number you hear becomes the starting point, and you never move far enough away from it.

Fritz Strack and Thomas Mussweiler published it in nineteen ninety seven, building on work by Tversky and Kahneman.

Stores use it every day. A crossed out high price makes the real price feel like a steal.

So before you negotiate, remember: whoever says a number first is already steering the answer.

The science

Based on the anchoring effect (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974; Strack and Mussweiler, 1997).

Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1974). Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases. Science, 185(4157), 1124-1131. Strack, F., & Mussweiler, T. (1997). Explaining the enigmatic anchoring effect. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 73(3), 437-446 (Gandhi anchors 9 and 140).
