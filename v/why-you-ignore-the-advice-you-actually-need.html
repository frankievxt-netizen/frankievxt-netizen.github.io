Why You Ignore the Advice You Actually Need

Harsher no-graffiti signs from more powerful sources drew more graffiti, a backlash psychologists call reactance.

Transcript

Someone tells you that you must stop, and suddenly you want it more. You are not being difficult.

In nineteen sixty six, psychologist Jack Brehm called this reactance. When a freedom feels threatened, we push back.

A decade later, James Pennebaker and Deborah Sanders tested it in restroom stalls at the University of Texas.

Some signs were mild. Others were harsh. Do not write on these walls under any circumstances.

Each sign was credited to either a high or a low campus authority.

The harsher the threat and the more powerful the source, the more graffiti people wrote on the signs.

Good advice can trigger the same push back. When it sounds like an order, part of you hears a threat.

So you do not hate good advice. You just want the choice to be yours.

The science

Based on psychological reactance (Brehm, 1966; Pennebaker and Sanders, 1976).

Brehm, J. W. (1966). A theory of psychological reactance. Academic Press. Pennebaker, J. W., & Sanders, D. Y. (1976). American graffiti: Effects of authority and reactance arousal. Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2(3), 264-267. High or low threats to the freedom to write on toilet stall walls were attributed to a high or low authority source; graffiti written on the placards rose with both threat level and authority. Exact sign wording is taken from textbook summaries of the study; the number of restrooms and the size of the difference were not confirmed. Single field study from the 1970s. Education only.
