Why You Keep Reading a Book You Hate

Theater fans who paid full price for season tickets went to more plays early on than fans who got a random discount.

Transcript

Page one hundred. The book is boring, and you keep reading anyway, because you already paid for it.

In nineteen eighty five, Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer tested this at the Ohio University theater.

Season ticket buyers were randomly given full price, a small discount, or a big discount.

In the first half of the season, full price buyers went to more plays than both discount groups.

Same shows. The only difference was money already spent. This is the sunk cost effect.

By the second half of the season, the gap had faded.

Later work adds nuance. A big review found the effect is real but varies a lot, and one economics experiment found it surprisingly small.

Still, the money is gone either way. Your evening is not. You are allowed to close the book.

The science

Based on the sunk cost effect (Arkes and Blumer, 1985; Roth, Robbert and Straus, 2015; Friedman et al., 2007).

Arkes, H. R., & Blumer, C. (1985). The psychology of sunk cost. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 35(1), 124-140 (Experiment 2: 54 Ohio University Theater season-ticket buyers, 1982-83 season, $15 vs. $13 vs. $8; mean tickets used in the first half 4.11 vs. 3.32 vs. 3.29, one-tailed p < .05; no significant difference in the second half). Roth, S., Robbert, T., & Straus, L. (2015). On the sunk-cost effect in economic decision-making: A meta-analytic review. Business Research, 8, 99-138 (effect present but heterogeneous). Friedman, D., Pommerenke, K., Lukose, R., Milam, G., & Huberman, B. A. (2007). Searching for the sunk cost fallacy. Experimental Economics, 10(1), 79-104 (small, erratic effect). The original field study was small and did not involve books. Education only.
