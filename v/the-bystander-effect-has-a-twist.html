The Bystander Effect Has a Twist

Crowds are supposed to make people ignore someone in trouble, but real camera footage tells a different story.

Transcript

You have probably heard that crowds make people ignore someone in trouble.

In nineteen sixty eight, John Darley and Bibb Latané staged a fake emergency over an intercom.

Alone, most people rushed to help. When they thought four others could hear, far fewer helped, and they were slower.

Psychologists call it diffusion of responsibility. Everyone assumes someone else will act.

You feel it when a stranger drops their groceries and you wait half a second to see who moves first.

But in twenty nineteen, researchers watched over two hundred real street fights caught on security cameras in three countries.

In about nine out of ten, at least one bystander stepped in. And the more people were around, the more likely someone helped.

So the crowd is not the problem. Waiting for the crowd is. Be the first one who moves.

The science

Based on the bystander effect (Darley and Latané, 1968; Philpot et al., 2020).

Darley, J. M., & Latané, B. (1968). Bystander intervention in emergencies: Diffusion of responsibility. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 8(4), 377-383. Philpot, R., Liebst, L. S., Levine, M., Bernasco, W., & Lindegaard, M. R. (2020). Would I be helped? Cross-national CCTV footage shows that intervention is the norm in public conflicts. American Psychologist, 75(1), 66-75.
